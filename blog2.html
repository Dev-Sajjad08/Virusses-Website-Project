HIV (Human Immunodeficiency Virus)

HIV is a virus that attacks the body's immune system, the defence that normally fights off infection. If left untreated it can lead to AIDS, the most advanced stage of the infection. Thanks to modern treatment, HIV has gone from a near-certain death sentence to a manageable, lifelong condition, but it remains a serious global health challenge.

Year Originated

HIV is believed to have crossed from chimpanzees to humans in the early twentieth century, but it was first recognised as a new disease in 1981, when unusual clusters of illness appeared in the United States. The virus itself was identified by scientists in 1983.

Location of Origin

Genetic studies trace the origin of HIV to Central Africa, in the region around the Democratic Republic of the Congo, where the virus is thought to have first passed from primates to humans before spreading worldwide during the twentieth century.

Spread of Virus / Disease

HIV is transmitted through specific body fluids: blood, semen, vaginal and rectal fluids, and breast milk. The main routes are unprotected sex, sharing needles or injecting equipment, and transmission from mother to child during pregnancy, birth, or breastfeeding.

Importantly, HIV does not spread through everyday contact. It cannot be passed on by hugging, sharing food, saliva, or insect bites. This matters, because stigma built on false beliefs about casual contact has caused real harm to people living with the virus.

Facts and Figures of the Damage

HIV remains one of the most significant pandemics in modern history. According to UNAIDS, around 39 million people worldwide are living with HIV, and the disease has caused over 40 million deaths since the epidemic began. With access to treatment, however, millions now live long, healthy lives.

Precautions Required

There is no cure for HIV yet, but it can be controlled with antiretroviral therapy (ART), which keeps the virus suppressed and prevents it from being passed on. Prevention tools include condoms, clean injecting equipment, and PrEP, a medicine taken by people at higher risk to prevent infection. Regular testing is also vital, since early diagnosis leads to far better outcomes.
